An Open Letter

On page 2 of the Folha de S.Paulo (FSP), journalist Fernando de Barros e Silva published a piece in his column SOS tucana (lowercase, as he wrote it) — a piece he knew full well would be read across the country and abroad, given that it appeared in a respected publication, the same one I was fortunate enough to write for as a columnist for a time.
Having spent years working in radio, print, and television as an economist, I know my way around a newsroom. That's why I believe in and respect opinion journalism: my views have always been treated with respect, and I extend the same to others. Opinion in the press is the editorial backbone on which freedom of the press rests, and through it, the health of democracy.
Whether it was because it happened to be Carnival Monday, or for some other reason I can't explain, Mr. Fernando crossed a line in a way that was unfounded, unnecessary, and frankly prejudiced.

Open Letter
Nazism revealed humanity's darkest face — a side of human nature that the world has been trying to forget ever since.
Some historians have studied Nazi politics as a case study, though they unanimously reject its extremism. The Nazi government did pull Germany out of the crisis it was in and gave it a sense of direction and hope. Hope that was then weaponized by those with malicious intent to deceive and destroy. I want to be absolutely clear: I do not support, in any way, the crimes committed by Nazi Germany. I condemn any and all crimes against humanity. But it is also true that I — a man steeped in Eastern culture — find meaning in the symbol of the swastika. Consider this: Vasco currently uses the symbol of the Maltese cross — an emblem with centuries of history behind it. I'm a Flamengo supporter. Does that mean I should resent that symbol because Vasco da Gama adopted it? Of course not. You have to be able to separate the two.
