Why work with projects

Clear up your doubts about how to use project-based learning in the classroom

Experts agree: working with projects is fascinating and surprising. Fascinating because it can engage even the most indifferent students. Surprising because it carries the seed of the unexpected. Below you'll find nine common questions on the topic, answered by three top experts. These are questions that trouble both those who have never ventured into this world – and those who are no longer beginners. Our guests are Regina Scarpa, general coordinator of the Center for Studies and Documentation for Community Action, in São Paulo; Vinicius Ítalo Signorelli, Natural Sciences teacher and member of the Parameters in Action program from the Ministry of Education; and Vera Grellet, project coordinator at Redeensinar. Many answers complement each other. Others reveal different points of view. Proof that in education, as you well know, there is no single, crystallized truth.

What does it mean to work with projects?

Vera Grellet It means starting from real, concrete, contextualized questions or situations that actually interest students. Understanding the problem situation is the project's goal. The actions and knowledge needed for understanding are discussed and planned between teacher and students. Everyone has tasks and responsibilities. It's like a trip: we are in Beijing (what we know) and we want to go to Shanghai (the path represents what we will learn). We must decide what to do, what to bring, and assign tasks. Along the way, we will also make new decisions.

How will the financial crisis affect the BRIC countries? The crisis has three broad impacts that will affect everyone, but to varying degrees. The first is a clear slowdown in the growth of demand for manufactured goods, especially in the United States and Europe, which will have a strong impact on China. The second impact, particularly strong for Russia but also affecting Brazil, is that the financial and banking crisis has led to a sharp reduction in credit for emerging economies. This has put downward pressure on the currencies of almost all countries, which have had to use foreign exchange reserves to offset the withdrawal of international bank financing. Russia's situation is particularly tricky because the Russian private sector borrowed heavily from the international financial system. Finally, huge fluctuations in the global economic cycle have triggered a significant adjustment in commodity prices. This will affect Russia due to oil, and also Brazil due to iron ore as well as many other commodities exported by the country. Are Brazil's foreign exchange reserves tough enough to face this crisis? Brazil's foreign exchange reserves are far higher than its short-term debt. Brazil's advantage is that, in addition to foreign exchange reserves, it can now access 30 billion dollars through the Federal Reserve currency swap line, and could potentially draw funds from the International Monetary Fund's short-term financing facility. Therefore, Brazil has at least 30 billion dollars and potentially up to 50 billion dollars of extra credit.

<Paulo Coelho interviewed by Veja magazine>
Enough with the magic
<V>– Your books talk less and less about occultism. Do you still consider yourself a magus?
<C> – The concept of a magus is more about a perception of the universe. It is a way of looking at the world that transcends concrete reality. But several of my books do not touch upon magic at all. My books discuss philosophical questions.
<V> – In your view, would that be their common intersection?
<C> – The common point is something called style. From my first book until now, I have always maintained an extremely direct, concise style. I keep stripping away, stripping away, until I approach the essence of things. In the beginning, this was misunderstood. People thought it was shallow. But it is precisely this characteristic that gave my books their unique edge.
<V> – After achieving such success, does criticism still bother you?
<C> – I am a deeply polarizing writer: people either love me or hate me. I am used to it. But the only criticism that hurts me is not directed at me personally. It is when someone says my readers are silly. I do not want to generalize, but cultural totalitarianism certainly exists in this country.
<V> – Do you feel rejected?
<C> – I think those who do not fit into a certain standard are rejected, a standard that only values things that are hard to understand and hard to access. Fortunately, this is only with critical theory; they are already detached from reality. The people who write these articles gather in ivory towers and do not know what is happening around them. They think they dominate the room, believing everyone is listening to them.
